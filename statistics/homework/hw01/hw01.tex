\section*{Homework 1: Data, Descriptive Statistics and Probability}
\noindent\textbf{Lecture 1.} Work on paper, by hand: a pen, a ruler and a basic calculator only. Show every step,
because the exam is on paper too and the method matters as much as the final number. Use the lecture's conventions: the
sample variance divides by $n-1$; quartiles are the medians of the lower and upper halves (leave the median out when
$n$ is odd); an outlier lies beyond $Q_1 - 1.5\,\mathrm{IQR}$ or $Q_3 + 1.5\,\mathrm{IQR}$.

\subsection*{Task 1. What kind of data?}
For each variable say whether it is \emph{nominal}, \emph{ordinal}, \emph{discrete} or \emph{continuous}, and whether
its mean would mean anything.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), itemsep=0pt]
  \item a student's postcode
  \item the number of siblings a student has
  \item the time a runner needs for 5\,km
  \item blood type (A, B, AB, O)
  \item a hotel rating: poor / fair / good / excellent
  \item the number of emails a person receives in a day
  \item the temperature at noon, in $^\circ$C
  \item T-shirt size: S, M, L, XL
\end{enumerate}
Then explain in one sentence why a variable written with digits (like (a)) can still be categorical.

\subsection*{Task 2. Where is the centre?}
The monthly rents (\$) of nine flats in one street are
\[ \hwRents. \]
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item Write the data as $x_1, \dots, x_9$ in the order given, and compute $\sum_{i=1}^{9} x_i$.
  \item Compute the mean, the median and the mode.
  \item How many of the nine flats are cheaper than the mean? Which of the three numbers describes a \emph{typical}
        flat in this street, and why?
  \item The most expensive flat is removed. Compute the new mean and median. Which one moved more, and why?
  \item The mean is above the median. What does that tell you about the shape of the data?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Task 3. Same mean, different spread}
Two delivery services brought five orders each. Delivery times in minutes:
\[ \text{Service A: } \hwDelA \qquad\qquad \text{Service B: } \hwDelB \]
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item Show that both services have the same mean.
  \item For each service make a table with columns $x_i$, $x_i - \bar{x}$ and $(x_i - \bar{x})^2$, and compute $s^2$ and
        $s$.
  \item Add up the column $x_i - \bar{x}$. What do you get, and why does that force us to square the deviations?
  \item You need a delivery for a meeting that starts in 30 minutes. Which service do you choose, and which number
        from (b) supports your choice?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Task 5. Your own data: sixty rolls of a die}
Roll an ordinary die \hwRolls{} times and record each result in the table with tally marks. Keep the results in the
order you rolled them as well: parts (d) and (f) need the order.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{tabular}{|c|p{5.5cm}|c|c|}
\hline
Face & Tally & Frequency & Relative frequency \\ \hline
1 & & & \\ \hline 2 & & & \\ \hline 3 & & & \\ \hline
4 & & & \\ \hline 5 & & & \\ \hline 6 & & & \\ \hline
Total & & \hwRolls & 1 \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item Draw a bar chart of the frequencies. Should the bars touch, as in a histogram? Explain.
  \item Write down the sample space $\Omega$ and the event $A=$ ``an even number''. Give the classical $P(A)$ and the
        empirical $P(A)$ from your rolls.
  \item How many times would you \emph{expect} each face in \hwRolls{} fair rolls? Which face is furthest from that, and
        do you think your die is unfair? (You will learn to test this properly in Lecture 10; here, just argue.)
  \item Split your rolls, in order, into \hwBlocks{} blocks of \hwBlock{}. For each block compute the share of even
        rolls, then the share in all \hwRolls. Which numbers lie further from $\tfrac12$: the blocks or the total? What
        does that tell you about empirical probability and the amount of data?
  \item From the frequency table, compute the mean and the sample variance of your \hwRolls{} rolls
        ($\bar x = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{f} f \cdot \text{frequency}_f$). Compare them with the mean and variance of a fair die,
        computed from its six equally likely faces.
  \item Ask someone to write down \hwRolls{} ``random'' die results from imagination, without rolling. Find the longest
        run of the same face in their list and in yours. Which is longer, and why do people who invent random numbers
        avoid runs?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Task 6. Probability rules}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item One card is drawn from a standard deck of 52. Find $P(\text{red} \cup \text{queen})$ with the addition rule.
        Which cards would you count twice without the correction?
  \item Two dice are rolled. List the sample space as a $6 \times 6$ grid and find $P(\text{sum} = 7)$ and
        $P(\text{sum} \ge 10)$.
  \item Find $P(\text{at least one six})$ for two dice twice: by counting cells in your grid, and with the complement
        rule. Then use the complement rule for four dice.
  \item One die, $A = \{2,4,6\}$. For each of $B=\{1,2,3\}$, $C=\{1,2,3,4\}$ and $D=\{1,3,5\}$, decide whether it is
        independent of $A$ and whether it is mutually exclusive with $A$. Show the numbers you compare.
  \item Use the complement rule to find the probability that at least two people share a birthday in a group of
        \hwBdayA{} and in a class of \hwBdayB{} (365 equally likely days, no leap years). Write the product out for the
        group of \hwBdayA.
  \item \hwCoins{} fair coins are tossed. List the whole sample space. Find $P(\text{exactly two heads})$, and
        $P(\text{at least one head})$ with the complement rule.
  \item In a class of \hwClass{} students, \hwPython{} study Python, \hwR{} study R, and \hwBoth{} study both. Draw a
        Venn diagram with the count in each region. A student is picked at random: find $P(\text{Python} \cup
        \text{R})$, $P(\text{neither})$ and $P(\text{Python only})$.
  \item A phone PIN has \hwPin{} digits, each 0--9, chosen at random. Find the probability that all \hwPin{} digits are
        different. Which problem from this homework uses the same idea?
  \item A card is drawn, put back, the deck is shuffled and a second card is drawn. Find $P(\text{both hearts})$ with
        the multiplication rule. Why may you multiply here?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Task 8. Explain it in words}
Answer each in three or four sentences.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item A small company has \hwStaff{} employees who each earn \$\hwStaffPay{} a month, and an owner who pays herself
        \$\hwOwnerPay{} a month. A newspaper writes: ``The average monthly pay at this company is almost
        \$\hwNewsClaim.'' Compute the mean and the median monthly pay of all \hwStaff{} + 1 people. Is the newspaper
        wrong? Which number would you report to someone who wants to work there, and why?
  \item A hospital reports a mean waiting time of \hwWaitMean{} minutes and a median of \hwWaitMedian{} minutes. What do
        the two numbers together tell you about the waiting times? Which number better describes what a typical
        patient experiences?
  \item Two classes both have a mean exam score of \hwClassMean. In class A the standard deviation is \hwClassSdA{}, in
        class B it is \hwClassSdB. Describe what each class's scores probably look like. In which class would you
        expect more students below 50?
  \item A weather app says ``70\,\% chance of rain tomorrow''. Is that a classical or an empirical probability? Explain
        where such a number could come from.
  \item A fair die has just shown \hwStreak{} sixes in a row. Your friend says the next roll ``must'' be something else.
        Another says sixes are ``hot'' and will keep coming. Who is right?
\end{enumerate}
